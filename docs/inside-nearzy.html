Nearzy

The shops you already walk past, open on your phone.

Most shopping apps ship to you from a warehouse in another state. Nearzy's catalogue is the shops on your own street — their real shelves, their real stock, ready in minutes rather than days.

The idea

Location isn't a filter here. It's the whole catalogue.

On an ordinary marketplace you search for a thing, then discover what it costs to get it to you. Nearzy works the other way round: you tell it where you are, and it shows you what the shops around you actually have on their shelves right now.

That one inversion changes everything downstream. Delivery is short because the seller is close. Stock is honest because it belongs to a shopkeeper who counted it. And the money stays in the neighbourhood, which is the part that makes a small shop want to join at all.

A shop three streets away can compete on the thing a national warehouse can never match: being three streets away.

Who it serves

Three kinds of people, one app.

Nearzy isn't three products. It's one app that recognises who has signed in and shows them their own version of it — which is why a shopkeeper needs nothing more than the phone already in their pocket.

How an order works

From tap to doorstep.

Six steps, and two of them exist purely to make sure nobody — shopper, shopkeeper or platform — has to take the others on trust.

  1. A shopper picks their spot Current location, or any point on the map. Everything they see from here on is filtered to what's reachable from it.
  2. They fill a cart from nearby shelves Possibly from more than one shop — the order keeps track of which items came from where, so each shopkeeper only ever sees their own lines.
  3. Nearzy prices the order, not the phone The app sends a list of items and quantities; the total is calculated on Nearzy's side from its own price list. A modified app can't invent a cheaper price.
  4. The shopper pays Razorpay handles the card, UPI, netbanking or wallet. Nearzy then re-checks the payment's signature with Razorpay before treating it as real.
  5. The shopkeeper packs and dispatches The order appears on their Today screen and moves forward one step at a time — it can't skip from placed straight to delivered.
  6. Stock comes off the shelf, or goes back on it Paying reduces the count automatically. Cancelling a paid order returns it, so the shelf figure stays true either way.

The part that runs itself

Two problems every small shop has, handled overnight.

This is the part a shopkeeper couldn't do alone with a notebook, and the reason to be on Nearzy rather than simply on the high street.

Knowing what's about to run out

A rule like "warn me under ten units" is useless in a marketplace. One shop sells three shawls a month; another sells forty loaves a day. The same threshold is either constant noise or a warning that turns up after the shelf is already bare.

So Nearzy measures days of cover instead: how long this shop's stock of this item will last at the rate it is actually selling. An item is flagged only when it is both running low and selling faster than its own recent normal — so a genuine rush gets noticed, and a slow-moving item sitting quietly does not cry wolf.

Selling today's stock today

Fresh stock unsold at closing time is a loss. Fresh stock discounted at seven in the evening is a sale. Nearzy runs that discount on a clock so the shopkeeper doesn't have to stand there rewriting labels.

Nothing here rewrites a price a shopkeeper didn't agree to. The opt-in and the floor are the whole point.

Getting a shop online

The reason shops say no, and what we did about it.

Ask any shopkeeper why they aren't on a marketplace and the answer is rarely about commission. It's that putting three hundred items online means three hundred forms — photograph it, name it, describe it twice, invent a code for it. That's days of unpaid work before a single sale, and it is where most apps like this quietly die.

So Nearzy reads the packet instead. The shopkeeper photographs a product and the name, brand, net quantity, category and description come back filled in. They check what's there, type the price, and it's live. Anything the photo didn't show clearly is flagged rather than guessed at, so nothing wrong slips through unnoticed.

One photo and a price, instead of seven fields. That is the difference between a shop signing up and a shop meaning to, later.

Two deliberate limits are worth stating. It never sets a price — that is always the shopkeeper's own number, because a wrong name is a nuisance they fix in a second while a wrong price costs them real money. And it drafts rather than publishes: nothing reaches the catalogue until a person has looked at it.

It works best on packaged goods, where the packet states what the thing is. Loose produce and unbranded local goods give thinner results, and the shopkeeper fills the gaps — which is still far less typing than starting from an empty form. Reading a photo takes a few seconds, and occasionally longer; if it is taking too long the form hands itself back rather than leaving anyone waiting.

Why it can be trusted

Four promises, and how each one is actually kept.

The shop is real
No shop can trade until a person on the platform team has approved it — with the owner's name and photo, PAN card, photo ID and business licence on screen while they decide. Approvals are recorded against the admin who made them.
The price is the real price
Totals are worked out on Nearzy's side from its own price list, never accepted from the phone. Money is held in whole paise throughout, so nothing drifts by a rounding error between the shelf label and the receipt.
The payment actually happened
Every payment is verified against Razorpay's own cryptographic signature before an order is treated as paid. If verification fails the order is marked failed rather than quietly confirmed, and the same payment can't be counted twice.
The account is yours
Signing in on a device creates a session that can be ended from anywhere — including every device at once. Sessions renew silently while in use, so people aren't asked for a password mid-shop, and a stolen session can be revoked without changing anything else.

Where it stands

What's built, and what's deliberately still open.

Nearzy works end to end today: a shopper can sign up, find a nearby shop, pay for something and watch it arrive, while the shopkeeper packs it and the platform team approves who's allowed to sell. What follows is an honest ledger, not a roadmap pitch.

Not yet known gaps

  • Shoppers can't write reviews Ratings are shown and already order the listings, but there's no way yet for a customer to leave one
  • Automation is switched off by default Deliberate — it changes prices, so it's opt-in per deployment until launch
  • Delivery is the shop's own No courier network or rider tracking; shopkeepers dispatch how they already do
  • Not yet hardened for scale Some groundwork remains before running with real customer data at volume

About the figures in the app. Nearzy is pre-launch. Everything currently visible in a demo — the shops, the customers, the orders, the ratings — is illustrative sample data, built to show how the product behaves. It should not be read as traction.

That sample catalogue is deliberately specific rather than generic: Kashmiri craft and produce — Kani shawls, saffron, walnut woodcraft, silk — alongside everyday grocery in Delhi, Mumbai and Bangalore. It's the mix the product was designed against, and a fair picture of the first market it's aimed at.
